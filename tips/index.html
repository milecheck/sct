Tips from walking it

What I wish I’d known, plus what the trail society and other hikers say.

The app Packing list Getting there Tips Local services Privacy Official trail site
Two kinds of tip below. Mine are from my own thru-hike — what I saw, filled, ate and got wrong. Reported ones come from the trail society (qPAWS) or other hikers, and I say so where they do. If the two disagree, I’ve left both in.

Water is the thing to plan around

Not gear, not fitness. Three of the best huts sit on summits with nothing running, and small creeks go dry in a hot August.

◆Tin Hat has no water at all. Not at the hut, not at the summit. Fill before the climb — Lewis Lake coming from the south end is the reliable one. Heading north from Tin Hat can run 7–8 km dry, further than people expect.
◆Walt Hill: guides say the nearest water is 15–20 minutes off. I found a spring about five minutes downhill with good water — but it’s seasonal either way, so don’t arrive empty in late summer.
◆Manzanita Bluffs has none. Wednesday Lake around km 12 is the last dependable fill before it. Three separate parties say the same.
◆Sarah Point is essentially dry. There’s a tiny signed creek about 200 m away, but it’s tiny — don’t plan a night on it. Get on the boat with every bottle full, and know the first few sources after the start are poor.
◆The guidebook prints a source that isn’t there, somewhere around km 19.5–21.5. Nobody in my party found it and I’ve not met anyone who has. Don’t plan on it.
◆Treat everything, including the rainwater barrels at huts.

Water reliability moves month to month, so this is orientation, not a list. The app carries every source with what’s actually known about it — including which ones are disputed and which nobody has ever checked.

The huts

◆No reservations. Free, first come, one night. qPAWS asks that bunks go to thru-hikers first, and that groups of eight or more tent instead.
◆Carry a tent anyway. Tin Hat is reachable by logging road, so summer weekends bring car campers — one July report counted thirty-plus people. A tent also lets you stop short or push past when the day goes sideways.
◆Dogs aren’t allowed inside. If you bring one, plan to tent every night, and make sure the tent has room for you and the dog both.
◆Play the week against the crowds. Hit the quiet huts on the weekend so the popular ones land midweek. Weekends are when the road- accessible huts fill with people who aren’t walking the trail.
◆Mice are a problem in all of the huts. Unless you want holes chewed through to your granola bars, put ALL food in the bear cache. Don’t ever leave food behind. Pack it in, pack it out.
◆Bear caches exist at most huts, not all. On my walk: caches at Rieveley Pond, Confederation Lake, Lewis Lake, Walt Hill, Fiddlehead Landing and the Troubridge emergency shelter. None at Anthony Island or Lois Lake, and I didn’t see one at the Troubridge hut. Carry cord and a dry bag and know how to hang before you need to.
◆Two worth skipping: Homestead around km 29 is a clearing between a road and a creek, and people who stayed wished they’d gone on to Rieveley’s Pond. Elephant Lake is damp with room for one or two tents — continue to Golden Stanley Hut.
◆Four huts have pellet stoves — Confederation Lake, Tin Hat, Walt Hill, Troubridge. qPAWS says not to depend on them working or being stocked.

Finding your way

◆Walk north to south. qPAWS built it that way — roughly twice the markers face you, and the signage is better. You also move with the flow instead of meeting everyone head-on at the huts. Walking the other way works too, and the app flips to match whichever way you go.
◆The markers are directional. Squares face you walking south, diamonds face north.
◆Pink ribbon means a reroute. Follow it. The old line has usually been logged over and can’t be walked anyway.
◆Don’t plan your day off the kilometre posts. The trail’s 178 marker kilometres are more like 183–187 of actual walking. Manzanita is marker km 16 and about 21 km from Sarah Point. Newer signs are better; older ones predate reroutes.
◆Places it’s easy to go wrong (all four are flagged on the app’s map): the big logging-road crossing between Spring Lake and Elk Lake, around km 95–97 — the trail goes back in uphill, not along the road. The Dixon Road exit after Walt Hill, around km 135 — unmarked, and people walk past it. Scout Mountain’s viewpoint bench, around km 46–50 — the path is the narrow one in front of it. And the stretch between Elk Lake and Walt Hill, km 110–122 — qPAWS abandoned the inside route, so old maps and routing apps still argue; follow the orange markers on the outside route.
◆Five huts sit off the through-line. Anthony Island (km 65) and Fiddlehead Landing (km 81) are short detours; Elk Lake (km 110) is signed two ways; Troubridge (km 158) is its own walk; Rainy Day Lake (km 169) is about 300 m downhill. Read the junction signs rather than the line on a screen.
◆Viewpoint loops rejoin the trail — Scout Mountain and the Sliammon Lakes loops are the known ones, and the official section maps show more between Manzanita and Powell River. At least one carries its own kilometre marker, so being off the line there doesn’t mean you’re lost.

Days, climbs and the season

◆Ignore anyone’s prescribed day count. I was told nine to eleven days. Mine took sixteen. Take the days you take.
◆The south end climbs more than people expect. Golden Stanley up to Troubridge is roughly a thousand metres before the long descent to Rainy Day. Fiddlehead Landing to Tin Hat is the hardest day per kilometre on the trail.
◆Snow lingers up high into early summer — Tin Hat, Walt Hill, Troubridge. It swings hard year to year: nearly clear one May, waist-deep the next.
◆Expect a fire ban in July and August. Fires are only ever allowed in existing pits with dead wood, and the Coastal Fire Centre bans them most high summers. Plan on the stove.
◆September empties out. Mid-May through August is the busy stretch, heavily so on the northern section. One September party met a single day hiker in six days.
◆Late-summer smoke can erase the viewpoints. One August party lost Tin Hat, Walt Hill and Troubridge entirely.
◆It’s a forest walk. Under 10 km of the 180 is actually along the ocean.

Food and resupply

◆You don’t have to carry it all. Drop pre-packed boxes at the Shingle Mill (right on the trail around km 50) or the Lang Bay Store (about 2.5 km off the Dixon Road crossing). Both have held hikers’ bags free. Call first — it’s a favour, not a service, and the storage isn’t secured.
◆Sunshine Coast Shuttle will place drops for you, but it’s pricey — one group paid $300 for a single mid-trail drop.
◆Buy food in Powell River, not on the way. Save-On-Foods and Quality Foods are both on the Joyce Avenue strip, about 25 minutes uphill from the Westview ferry. Freeze-dried pouches at the outdoor shop run $15–23 each — order those before you come.
◆Bring your dog’s food with you. Town won’t stock their brand. And it’s heavy — switch to a sport formula before the trip and bring peanut butter for calories.
◆The Shinglemill Pub is the mid-trail meal. The trail comes off Scout Mountain right at it.

Getting rides, and splitting them

◆Shuttles are expensive alone and reasonable shared. One pair paid $375 privately from Saltery Bay to Sarah Point, and were quoted about $105 each to join another party going the same day. Call and ask to be put on an existing run — that’s the whole trick.
◆Sarah Point has no dock. It’s a rocky bluff, and vehicles only get within about 500 m. Lund Water Taxi (604-483-9749) drops there on request and takes well-behaved dogs free, tide permitting. Terracentric runs a flat $225 + GST for up to four, at any tide, but no dogs.
◆The bus takes no dogs — BC Transit, verified the hard way. It also doesn’t run Thursdays.
◆Treat the ferries as a trip leg of their own. Earls Cove ran an hour late both ways for one party, there’s no food there, and Mondays and Fridays are slammed. Build a travel day at each end.
◆Parking: free but unsecured at the Saltery Bay kiosk, Lang Bay, Dixon Road and in town. Lund’s lot by the water taxi is about $13 a day with a two-day minimum. Shingle Mill has free roadside parking — the road, not the pub lot.

Your phone will not save you

◆Expect no service between Powell River and about Lang Bay. No carrier fixes it. Tell your family that before you go, so silence doesn’t read as trouble.
◆Signal, where it exists, is on high points — Tin Hat, Manzanita, Walt Hill, Troubridge, Rainy Day Lake. Hikers also report a bar at Rieveley Pond, Fairview and much of the last stretch into Saltery Bay. Treat any single spot as luck.
◆Airplane mode from trailhead to town. Out of coverage a phone burns itself down hunting for towers. GPS still works in airplane mode.
◆Don’t depend on a tracking app. Mine came out with gaps and bad distances, and the phone was dead a lot. Nice to have, not something to navigate by.
◆Leave a trip plan with someone — route, hut-by-hut dates, who’s with you, and a hard check-in deadline. If it passes, their job is to call 911. Rescue in BC is free and they’d rather come early than late.

Feet, body, camp

◆Tape a hot spot the second you feel it. Stop, pack off, dry the skin, tape. Not at the next break — that’s how it becomes a blister. Pre-cut strips at home; never wrap all the way around a toe, and never tape over broken skin.
◆Poles change the descents. Lengthen them before a long one, plant ahead so the pole loads before your foot lands. There’s real research behind it — measurably less force through the knee.
◆Nothing dries overnight here. Coastal nights are muggy even without rain. Dry things in the morning sun or at a sunny break instead, and keep wet clothes and boots out of the tent — the moisture ends up on your sleeping bag.
◆Fleece over down for the mid-layer. Down quits when it’s damp. Keep down as a dry camp layer if you bring it.
◆Check yourself for ticks after the walk — the season runs March into October here.
◆Bear spray within reach, not buried. Bears, cougars and wolves all live along the trail. Expect sign more than animals — one party saw plenty of scat and nothing else. Buy it in Powell River; it can’t fly, and neither can stove fuel.
◆Swim. Wednesday, Confederation, Elk and Rainy Day all deliver, the last two with docks. It’s the best thing on the trail.

Town, at either end

◆Nancy’s Bakery in Lund — the cinnamon buns are the reason everyone mentions it. Time your boat around it.
◆Townsite Brewing is the post-hike beer, in the 1939 art-deco building in the historic Townsite.
◆Laundry: Westview Dry Cleaners and Laundromat on Joyce Avenue, self-serve or drop-off. Don’t wash your clothes in the lakes.
◆Showers: Haywire Bay campground has coin showers, a couple of dollars.
◆Leave a donation. qPAWS suggests about $5 a day per hiker for hut and trail upkeep. Volunteers built every one of these huts and cut every blowdown. Budget it like a fee.
◆The printed guidebook and map are sold at the qathet Visitor Centre and nowhere online. Worth having — but see the water source above that doesn’t exist.

If you have a spare day: Savary Island

Fifteen minutes by water taxi from Lund, white sand and the warmest ocean swimming you’ll get in this province. In July and August the boat leaves Lund roughly hourly from 9 am, back from Savary on the half hour — confirm your return when you book, because the last one is early evening and the schedule moves.

◆Walk-on only. Leave the car in Lund. A land taxi meets the boat if you need to get across the island, or bring a bike for the sand roads.
◆Bring everything. No ATMs, no public washrooms, no water, no garbage. Pack it in and out.
◆Nowhere public to sleep. Most of the island is private, and Sunshine Coast Tourism actively discourages day-tripping because the amenities are so thin. Go knowing that, or don’t go.
◆The dunes are 10,000 years old — the best coastal dune ecosystem in western Canada, per the island’s land trust. Stay off them.
