Sunshine Coast Trail packing list

What I actually carried, and the few things this trail asks for that others don’t.

Almost everyone I met on the trail overpacked, the pack weighed them down, and they left the extra at Shingle Mill — me included, I left a pile of clothes there. To avoid the mistake: here’s what I think you really need, and what I’d leave behind.

What this trail asks for specifically

◆Capacity for 3 litres of water. Not because you carry three all day — because two of the huts are dry camps, and the stretch over Tin Hat can be long and hot with nothing running.
◆A filter you trust. Lakes and creeks, not taps. Some sources are seasonal and some are questionable even in reports.
◆A way to store food away from bears. Some huts have a cache. Some don’t, and a cache can be full or broken when you arrive — carry your own plan.
◆A sleeping pad even if you plan on huts. The huts are first-come and free to anyone who reaches them. If one is full, you’re on the floor or in a tent.
◆A pack under 40 pounds, all-in. The usual rule is a loaded pack at no more than about 20% of your body weight — for most people that lands at 25–35 lb. 40 lb is the ceiling, not the target. Water is where it sneaks up: 3 litres for a dry-camp night is 6.6 lb on its own.
◆More food than the kilometres suggest. The marker posts read shorter than the walking. Plan by days, not by distance.

The list

The pack itself

  • 50–60 litres is the range for this trail. I carried 55 with a tent in it, and it was right
  • A great pack: an Osprey 50 L — the comfort under a heavy load is what you’re paying for. I’d look at their 60 too
  • A more budget-friendly one: the 55 L I actually used — budget friendly, and it did the whole trail with a tent in it
  • Check it comes with a pack liner — most do, and it saves buying one

Sleep

  • Sleeping bag rated for the coldest night you expect, not the average one. A −7°C (20°F) down bag — a clear August night up at the huts still gets cold
  • An ultralight liner — adds a bit of warmth, and it is the thing you actually wash when you get home instead of the bag
  • Sleeping pad — the huts have wooden platforms
  • A quiet pad, if you have the choice. A crinkly inflatable on a wooden platform wakes the whole hut every time someone rolls over — it was my biggest complaint about hut nights. When I replace mine it’ll be the NeoAir XLite NXT or the NEMO Tensor All-Season — both built not to crinkle. They’re light too: mine felt heavier than my tent, and a budget pad can be — the pad is where an upgrade buys back the most weight
  • Tent or tarp if you’re not willing to gamble on hut space. A one-person backpacking tent is the lighter answer; a two-person is the one you want if there are two of you or a dog. Every hut has tent space beside it, so this is insurance rather than a different trip
  • A pocket tarp — under the tent to keep it dry, or in front of it to keep dirt out. Somewhere to step and to put things down that isn’t dirt
  • Earplugs for sleeping. Huts are shared, and other people’s pads crinkle too.

Water and food

  • Filter or purifier, plus a backup method. A gravity filter — hang it and walk away
  • Bottles/bladder totalling about 3 L of capacity. Collapsible soft flasks — pack down to nothing when empty
  • Stove, fuel, pot, spork — or a genuinely no-cook plan. Budget: the stove-and-pot set I carried
  • Food for your planned days plus one spare day
  • Food bag and cord if you’re hanging rather than trusting a cache
  • Smell-proof bags for food and garbage
  • Coffee or tea bags — a warm cup of anything is great morning and evening

Food

  • Freeze-dried meals are the easy answer — boil water, wait, eat. Backpacker’s Pantry oatmeal for breakfast, Mountain House for dinner
  • Check the servings-per-pouch number, not the calories on the front. A “two serving” pouch is one dinner after a 25 km day
  • Buy them before you come. The same pouches are $15–23 each at Powell River Outdoors — fine for the one you forgot, brutal for a two-week resupply. Ordering ahead in bulk is the single biggest saving on this whole list
  • You don’t have to carry it all. Drop pre-packed resupply boxes at the Shingle Mill or the Lang Bay Store before you start — both sit right where the trail meets town. Sunshine Coast Shuttle will place boxes for you too, but it’s pricey
  • More of this section to come — I’m still writing up what I actually ate and what I got wrong

Clothing

  • Everything you buy should be quick-dry — shirts, shorts, socks, all of it. This is the coast; wet things need to dry overnight
  • Rain shell that actually works. This is the coast.
  • Insulating layer for hut evenings — something thin that stuffs away small. A sweater takes up way too much room; I learned that the hard way. Mine is a Kühl jacket, which they sell direct rather than on Amazon, so there’s nothing to click here — the thing that matters is thin and compressible, not the brand
  • These shorts — I lived in them. Also high-waisted biker shorts
  • Hiking shirt and shorts/trousers, plus dry sleep layers you never hike in. A UPF 50 long-sleeve sun shirt — beats reapplying sunscreen
  • Two or three pairs of wool socks — wool is great here — and accept that some will be wet. These blister-proof ones I loved, and the anti-blister compression socks I wore
  • Swimsuit — the trail sleeps beside lakes most nights, and a swim at the end of a hot day is the best thing on it
  • Sunglasses. I love these polarised ones for $30 — the arms are grippy and they stay on well. AI glasses like these Meta ones ($299) snap quick pics and videos — with poles in both hands and a dog, the phone stays buried. Off inside the huts
  • Waterproof touchscreen gloves — I carried them in summer and used them
  • A warm hat in the shoulder seasons — in summer I never needed one

Feet

  • Broken-in boots or trail runners — there is mud, root and rock. The Merrell Moab 3 waterproof mids I wore
  • Gaiters — optional. People love them with trail runners or anything low-cut; with mids or boots you don’t need them
  • Camp shoes worth their weight after ten hours
  • Blister kit you know how to use

Navigation and safety

  • Bear spray and butane canisters can’t go on a plane — not in checked bags either. If you’re flying in, buy both in Powell River: Powell River Outdoors is right by the ferry. Leave the spray behind at the end
  • This app, offline, with your phone in airplane mode
  • A battery bank — there is nowhere to charge for days, and unfortunately it’s heavier than one would like. A solar bank helps, but I had a hard time getting a charge — the trail is under canopy most of the day. Size the battery as if there were no sun
  • Headlamp plus spare batteries
  • First aid kit, including anything you personally need. Add a mini super glue — it closes small splits in skin, gear, and a dog’s paw pad
  • Mini sewing kit — get one with thick waxed thread — what tears is a strap, not a shirt
  • Your own medications, for the full trip plus a spare day
  • Ibuprofen. Everyone wants it by day three
  • Strong DEET repellent — the small bottle is plenty
  • Women: consider bringing UTI medication or test strips. They’re common out there and there is no pharmacy between the trailheads
  • Contacts and solution, or spare glasses
  • Bear spray. Remember it can’t fly, so if you are flying in, stop in at Powell River Outdoors to purchase
  • Satellite messenger if you’re solo. There is no cell coverage for most of the trail and 911 needs a signal. The Garmin inReach Mini 2 is the one most people carry. Budget for the subscription too
  • Check which Garmin you have. A Garmin fitness watch tracks your day and will not call anyone. Satellite SOS is the inReach line — don’t confuse the two

Small things that mattered

  • Trekking poles — the descents are long. Budget pair
  • Quick-dry microfibre towel — mine was the big beach size, which was more towel than I needed; I’ll link a lighter one when I find it. Plus biodegradable body wipes for the days there is no lake
  • Trowel, and whatever else you need to bury it properly
  • Hand sanitiser, toothbrush and toothpaste, the short version of everything
  • Nail clippers — for sure. Sixteen days is long enough to need them
  • A mini detangling brush, if you have hair to fight with
  • Mini medical scissors — the blunt-tipped first-aid kind. Used them every day
  • A small razor, if that’s your thing
  • Mini deodorant — a spray, so it works on clothes too. Scented, so it caches at night with the food
  • Sunscreen, SPF lip balm, and a hat — the logging-road sections have no shade at all
  • For your face specifically, the one I used — buy the small size. Body sunscreen in your eyes is why people stop reapplying
  • Dry bag — it doubles as your bear-cache bag. An ultralight food bag works, or the dry bag you already own — I used my paddleboarding one
  • Pack liner — separate from the dry bag, and your pack should come with one. “Waterproof” pack covers are optimistic.
  • Camp suds — one small bottle of biodegradable soap
  • A few metres of paracord — clothesline at the hut, gear repairs, spare lace

If you’re bringing a dog

  • Their food, plus their own water and a bowl. Dog food is heavy — switch slowly to a sport formula before the trail, and bring peanut butter for the calories
  • Check every leg of your transport — BC Transit doesn’t allow pets
  • Paw protection — the rock is sharp and the road sections get hot. Paw wax is easier to keep on than boots
  • Their rabies vaccination card, on you — not left in the car
  • Dog-safe insect repellent — works for both of you

Against the 40 lb ceiling that leaves ~15 lb for food — at 1.5–2 lb per day, about 8–9 days’ worth, which is why resupplying in town beats carrying a whole thru-hike at once.

Water rides on top of all of it and swings through the day — a litre is 2.2 lb, and a 3-litre dry-camp carry is 6.6 lb over whatever the table says.

What I’d leave behind

Gaiters, a second cook pot, spare “town clothes”, and most of the just-in-case pile. The trail is long enough that every extra pound is paid for on the climbs.

A hammock, specifically. I carried one and never used it — the huts have platforms and the good trees are already spoken for. Leave it.

And if you get out there and realize you brought too much anyway: the Shingle Mill pub will hold excess gear while you finish the trail. Ask, don’t assume — but that’s what I’d do with a too-heavy pack rather than carry it to Saltery Bay.
