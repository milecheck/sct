Packing for the Sunshine Coast Trail

◆Containers for 4–5 litres of water. Carry the full capacity for dry camps and the Tin Hat dry stretch.
◆Water filter and backup treatment. Sources include lakes and creeks. Some are seasonal and many remain unchecked.
◆Your own food-storage setup. Some huts have no bear cache. Existing caches may be full or damaged.
◆A sleeping pad and tent. Hut platforms are first-come and may be full.
◆Weigh the loaded pack. Include food and dry-camp water. Three litres of water weighs 3 kg (6.6 lb).
◆Plan food by hiking days. Reroutes make some marker intervals longer or shorter than 1 km.

What I carried

This was my setup for a 2026 thru-hike. Choose gear for your fit, season and route.

Some links below are Amazon affiliate links. As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases.

Pack and sleep

  • I carried a IX INOXTO 55 L pack and a two-person tent for me and my dog.
  • I used a −7°C (20°F) sleeping bag, liner and inflatable pad. Hut bunks are wooden platforms.
  • My pad was heavy and loud on hut platforms. I would replace it with a lighter, quieter one.
  • I carried a tent because hut platforms are first-come. Check camping options for each planned stop.

Water and food

  • I carried containers with 3 L total capacity and filled them according to the next dry stretch. That is less than this trail asks for. Manzanita and Tin Hat are dry camps, and the note on Tin Hat says 4–5 L, or 6 L above 24°C, because the top is exposed. Carry the larger number rather than mine.
  • I filtered water from lakes, creeks and hut barrels, and carried a backup treatment method for when a filter clogs or freezes.
  • Some huts have no cache. Existing caches may be full or damaged. Bring your own food-storage setup.
  • Plan food by days and include one extra day. Confirm any resupply arrangement directly before leaving.

Cooking

  • I cooked on a canister stove and carried one fuel canister for the trip. Stove, pot and fuel came to about 1.5 lb.
  • I carried a second cook pot and never used it. One pot is enough for one person.
  • The only fuel stocked anywhere near the route is in Powell River. Carry what the whole trip needs, and buy the canister after you land — airlines do not carry them in checked or cabin baggage.

Clothing and feet

  • Quick-dry hiking layers, dry sleep layers, a rain shell and a thin insulating layer.
  • I carried two or three pairs of wool socks. Carry blister care and treat hot spots early.
  • I wore Merrell Moab 3 waterproof mids. Break in your footwear before the hike.
  • I used gloves in July and carried a swimsuit for lake swims.

Navigation and safety

  • Download offline maps before leaving signal and carry backup route information.
  • Much of the trail is under tree cover. Carry a battery bank and do not rely on solar charging.
  • Carry a headlamp, first-aid kit, repair kit and your own medications.
  • Carry bear spray where you can reach it and know how to use it. Bear spray and fuel cannot travel on a commercial flight, including checked baggage.
  • I used a watch to record my route. A satellite communicator can send an SOS without cellular service. Confirm its subscription is active before leaving.

Small items

  • Trekking poles, a quick-dry towel, trowel, hand sanitiser, sunscreen, insect repellent, lip balm and a hat.
  • Pack liner, dry food bag, cord, tape, blunt-tip scissors and a small sewing kit.
  • Use small amounts of biodegradable soap at least 60 m (200 ft) from lakes and streams.

Dog

  • Bring the dog’s food, water capacity, bowl, leash, sleep setup and first-aid supplies. DEET repellents are toxic to dogs. Ask your vet about nutrition, medication and insect protection.
  • I carried dog paw wax. Check paws during road and rock sections.
  • As of August 2026, BC Transit allows only small pets in a secure handheld carrier. Confirm each shuttle, taxi, boat and accommodation will accept your dog.

Loading the pack

Heavy things go in the middle, against your back. Weight that sits far from your spine pulls you backward on the climbs.

◆Bottom. Sleeping bag and camp clothes. Bulky, light, and not needed until camp.
◆Middle, against your back. Food, cook kit and water. On a dry stretch the water is the heaviest thing you carry: 4–5 L is 4–5 kg.
◆Top. Rain shell, warm layer, filter, first-aid kit and toilet kit. Things you want before camp.
◆Lid and hip-belt pockets. Phone, snacks, headlamp, sunscreen and repellent.
◆Where your hand can reach it. Bear spray, on a hip belt or shoulder strap. Not inside the pack.
◆Line the pack. A pack liner keeps the sleeping bag dry when the coast rains. Fill gaps with soft things so the load doesn’t shift.

Wearing the pack

The hip belt should carry most of the weight. Your shoulders steady the load. Loosen every strap, put the pack on, then tighten in this order.

1Hip belt. Centre the padding on your hip bones and pull it snug.
2Shoulder straps. Snug, not tight. They should wrap your shoulders with no gap at the top.
3Load lifters. The short straps above your shoulders. Pull gently, to about a 45° angle.
4Sternum strap. About 2–3 cm (an inch) below your collarbones.
5Adjust as you go. Loosen the load lifters a little on the descents, and snug the hip belt again after a break.

A pack only fits if its torso length matches yours. Have it measured before you buy.

What I’d leave behind

I carried gaiters, a second cook pot, spare town clothes and a hammock. I did not use them.

Ask Shingle Mill Pub before leaving gear there.
